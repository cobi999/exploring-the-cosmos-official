COSMOS · HUMANITY · PERSPECTIVE

Our Place in the Universe

Are humans really that special? The universe may have other plans.

A personal reflection on humanity and the cosmos

For decades, human beings have been made to believe that, somehow, we are the most special creatures — not only on Earth, but perhaps in the universe at large.

For years, we have even held beauty pageants in which the winner is crowned Miss Universe. Strange, isn’t it?

I am not saying this to be negative, but declaring ourselves the best in the universe when we have barely explored the cosmic ocean around us seems like an extraordinary display of entitlement.

How can we be so sure that there are no other civilizations out there? What if other intelligent beings exist — beings more technologically advanced than us, perhaps even possessing forms of beauty and intelligence that we cannot begin to imagine?

Humanity’s confidence in its own superiority has always fascinated me.

Look at Earth today. Human beings dominate the planet in ways no other species can. We have developed mathematics, built cities, invented computers, constructed aircraft, and sent spacecraft beyond our home world. Our technological achievements are remarkable.

But perhaps we should pause and ask ourselves a question: Are we truly the most extraordinary form of life that can exist, or are we simply the most extraordinary form of life we currently know?

Consider our relationship with other primates. Humans and chimpanzees share approximately 98–99% of their DNA, depending on how the comparison is made. Yet, despite that genetic similarity, the differences in our technological and cultural achievements are enormous.

Now imagine another civilization somewhere in the universe — one that evolved from a different biological history and developed intelligence far beyond our own.

What if the gap between that civilization and humanity were comparable, in some meaningful sense, to the gap we perceive between ourselves and other primates?

To such beings, we might not appear to be the masters of the universe. We might appear primitive.

Of course, we have no evidence that such a civilization exists, and DNA comparisons cannot tell us how intelligence would develop in an entirely different species. But the possibility invites an uncomfortable question: what makes us so certain that we occupy the highest position on the cosmic ladder?

Perhaps our greatest mistake is confusing being exceptional with being unsurpassable.

The Dinosaurs Who Ruled Before Us

Scientific evidence tells us that Earth has changed dramatically over billions of years. There was a time when the planet could not support life as we know it. Eventually, life emerged, diversified, and transformed the world.

Today, we look at Earth and see a planet shaped by human activity. It is tempting to assume that this is the final form of its story.

But why should we?

Millions of years ago, dinosaurs dominated terrestrial ecosystems. For an immense period of Earth’s history, they were among the most successful animals ever to inhabit the planet.

Imagine, for a moment, that dinosaurs possessed the ability to reflect on their place in the world. Imagine them looking at their surroundings and believing that their dominance was permanent.

Perhaps they would have planned for the future, just as we do today. Perhaps they would have imagined their descendants ruling the Earth indefinitely.

We cannot know what dinosaurs thought or whether they possessed anything resembling our understanding of the future. This is an imaginative exercise, not a claim about their actual minds.

But the point remains: dominance at one moment in history does not guarantee dominance forever.

Around 66 million years ago, a massive asteroid struck Earth, triggering environmental changes that led to the extinction of all non-avian dinosaurs. Birds, however, are living descendants of theropod dinosaurs, so dinosaurs did not disappear entirely.

The world changed. The ecosystems that had supported many of the great dinosaurs were transformed, and mammals eventually diversified into many of the forms we see today.

Much later, human beings emerged.

The creatures that once dominated the planet could never have guaranteed what would come after them. And neither can we.

What If the Asteroid Had Never Struck?

Here is a question that fascinates me: if that asteroid had never struck Earth, would human beings exist today?

We cannot know the exact alternative history. But the extinction event dramatically changed the course of evolution, opening ecological opportunities for mammals that had previously lived alongside dinosaurs.

Without that event, the evolutionary path leading to modern humans might never have unfolded as it did.

Think of your own existence. If your grandparents had never met, the precise chain of events that led to your birth might never have occurred. A small change in the past can have enormous consequences for the future.

The asteroid is a powerful example of how contingency shapes the history of life. Our existence is not evidence that the universe had to produce us. It is evidence that a particular chain of events eventually produced us.

I sometimes think of the end of the non-avian dinosaurs as a doorway through which a different chapter of Earth’s history emerged.

But we must be careful not to interpret this as proof that the extinction happened for humanity. Evolution has no known predetermined destination, and the asteroid did not strike Earth in order to create us.

We are here because of the history that unfolded — not necessarily because that history was leading toward us.

When we look at the planet today, it is easy to imagine that human beings represent the pinnacle of evolution.

We build machines, explore space, manipulate our environment, and contemplate our own origins. We can look billions of years into the past and develop theories about the future.

These are extraordinary abilities.

But evolution does not promise that every generation will become more intelligent, more complex, or more powerful than the one before it. Natural selection favours traits that help organisms reproduce in particular environments. Sometimes those traits involve greater complexity; sometimes simpler organisms thrive remarkably well.

There is no scientific law stating that humans must remain Earth’s dominant species, nor is there evidence that evolution is working toward a predetermined final form.

Future life on Earth could look very different from life today. Some existing species may disappear. Others may diversify. New forms of life could emerge over millions of years, provided the necessary conditions exist.

Perhaps descendants of today’s organisms will develop abilities we cannot imagine. Perhaps humans will change through biological evolution, technological modification, or some combination of both. Perhaps environmental changes will make our species vulnerable.

These are possibilities, not predictions.

What we do know is that Earth has never been static. Its atmosphere, continents, climate, ecosystems, and inhabitants have changed repeatedly.

Why, then, should we assume that the present arrangement is permanent?

Are We Really That Special?

Perhaps humanity is special in ways that genuinely matter. We possess remarkable capacities for language, cooperation, scientific inquiry, creativity, and reflection. We can study the stars and ask whether anyone else is looking back.

But being special does not necessarily mean being superior to every possible form of life.

We have not established whether intelligent extraterrestrial civilizations exist. We have not explored most of the universe, and our knowledge of life beyond Earth remains incomplete.

Our position is therefore unusual: we know enough to recognise how extraordinary our existence is, but not enough to determine whether it is unique.

That uncertainty should inspire humility rather than arrogance.

Maybe there are civilizations far older than ours. Maybe intelligence has emerged elsewhere in forms that would challenge our understanding of what it means to be alive. Or perhaps intelligent life is extraordinarily rare, and humanity represents one of the few places where the universe has become capable of reflecting upon itself.

At present, we do not know.

And perhaps admitting that we do not know is one of the most important steps toward understanding our place in the cosmos.

The dinosaurs once dominated Earth. Today, humans do. In the distant future, our descendants — or entirely different forms of life — may inhabit a world we would scarcely recognise.

So I return to the question that started this reflection:

Are humans meant to dominate Earth forever, or are we simply one part of a much longer story?

Perhaps we are not the final chapter.

Perhaps we are not even close. And perhaps the most remarkable thing about humanity is not that we have conquered our world, but that we have begun to question whether we are as important as we once believed.

We have looked into the universe and discovered that Earth is only one planet orbiting one star among hundreds of billions of stars in our galaxy, within a universe containing vast numbers of galaxies.

We do not yet know whether we are alone.

Until we do, perhaps we should be careful about crowning ourselves the champions of a universe we have barely begun to understand.

After all, how can we claim to be the greatest beings in existence when we have not yet discovered who else might exist?
